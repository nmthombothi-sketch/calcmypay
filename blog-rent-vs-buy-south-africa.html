Rent vs Buy in South Africa: Which is Better?

Jun 20267 min readAffordability

"Rent is dead money" is one of the most repeated phrases in South African personal finance — and it is only half true. Buying builds equity, but it also comes with costs renters never pay. Here is how to think about the decision honestly.

The true cost of buying

A bond repayment is only part of the cost of owning. Add these and the picture changes:

  • Transfer duty and registration costs (8–10% upfront on the purchase)
  • Rates and taxes to the municipality
  • Levies if it is a sectional title or estate
  • Building insurance
  • Maintenance and repairs — budget about 1% of the property value per year

The case for renting

Renting is not just "throwing money away". It offers flexibility to move for work, no exposure to interest rate hikes, no maintenance bills, and it frees up the large deposit and transfer costs to invest elsewhere — potentially earning more than property appreciation.

When buying makes sense

  • You plan to stay put for at least 5–7 years (long enough to recoup transfer costs)
  • You have a stable income and a solid emergency fund
  • You can afford the bond comfortably within the 30% rule
  • You have saved a deposit to reduce the bond

When renting makes sense

  • Your job or life situation might change in the next few years
  • You are still building your emergency fund and paying off debt
  • Property prices in your area are very high relative to rent
  • You would rather invest the deposit in the markets

A simple way to decide

Compare the total monthly cost of buying (bond + rates + levies + insurance + maintenance) against equivalent rent. If buying costs far more per month, renting and investing the difference may leave you wealthier. The break-even calculation most people skip

Buying only becomes financially better than renting once you have owned long enough to recoup the substantial upfront transaction costs — transfer duty, bond registration, and conveyancing fees typically totalling 8-10% of the purchase price. For most South African property markets, this break-even point sits somewhere between 5 and 7 years of ownership, meaning buying with a shorter time horizon in mind is often the more expensive choice once all costs are considered.

What renting genuinely offers beyond flexibility

Beyond the commonly cited flexibility to relocate, renting also transfers maintenance risk and cost entirely to the landlord, removes exposure to interest rate increases on a bond, and frees up the capital that would otherwise sit in a deposit — capital that could instead be invested and potentially grow faster than property values in your specific area.

The emotional versus financial decision

Buying a home carries genuine non-financial value for many people — stability, the ability to renovate freely, a sense of permanence — that a purely financial analysis cannot fully capture. It is worth being honest about how much weight these factors carry for you personally, since a financially "suboptimal" choice to buy can still be the right one if the non-financial benefits genuinely matter to your life.

A simple framework for deciding

  • Buy if: you plan to stay 5+ years, have a stable income, a solid emergency fund, and a genuine deposit saved
  • Rent if: your situation may change in the next few years, you are still building savings, or property in your target area is unusually expensive relative to rent

The break-even timeline worth knowing

Financial planners often cite a rough rule that buying only makes sense over renting if you plan to stay in the property for at least 5-7 years, since transaction costs (transfer duty, legal fees) need time to be offset by the equity built and any property appreciation.

The break-even point worth calculating

A useful exercise before deciding is calculating your specific break-even period — the number of years you would need to stay in a purchased property before the total cost of buying (including transfer costs) becomes cheaper than continuing to rent an equivalent property. This is often longer than people initially assume, frequently 5-7 years once all costs are included.

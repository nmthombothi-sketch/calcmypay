How is a Second Job Taxed in South Africa?

Jun 20266 min readSARS 2027 Tax Year

How having two jobs or a side income affects your tax in South Africa, and why you might owe SARS at year-end.

Quick answer: Income from a second job is added to your main income and taxed at your marginal rate. Because each employer only taxes their portion, you may owe SARS when you file.

How Two Incomes Are Taxed

SARS taxes your total combined income. The problem is each employer applies tax tables as if their salary is your only income, so not enough tax is deducted overall.

Why You Might Owe SARS

Because both employers under-deduct, your combined income may fall in a higher bracket than either job alone, leaving a shortfall you must settle when you file your return.

How to Avoid a Surprise

You can ask one employer to deduct additional PAYE, or set money aside. Registering for a SARS tax directive can help apply the correct rate.

A concrete illustration of the gap

If your main job pays R25,000/month and a second role pays R10,000/month, each employer taxes their portion as though it were your only R25,000 or R10,000 income respectively. But your true combined annual income of R420,000 sits in a higher bracket than either amount alone, meaning the combined PAYE actually withheld across both jobs is very likely less than what you will genuinely owe.

Proactive options to avoid a surprise tax bill

  • Request a tax directive from SARS — this instructs one employer to apply a different, more accurate withholding rate based on your combined income
  • Set aside a buffer voluntarily — estimate the likely shortfall and save toward it rather than being caught unprepared
  • File early and review the outcome — understanding whether you owe money as soon as possible gives you more time to arrange payment if needed

This is not a penalty, simply a timing mismatch

It is important to understand that owing money after having two jobs is not a punishment or an error — it reflects the genuine tax you owe on your combined income, which the monthly withholding system was never designed to calculate perfectly across multiple simultaneous employers.

Why two employers rarely withhold the correct total tax

Each employer calculates PAYE independently, applying the tax tables as though the salary they pay you is your only income for the year. When you have two employers, both apply this same assumption simultaneously — meaning your combined income is very likely under-taxed at source, since neither employer sees or accounts for the other income stream.

A concrete illustration of the shortfall

If you earn R15,000/month from a primary job and R8,000/month from a second role, each employer calculates PAYE as though that specific salary were your total annual income. In reality, your combined R23,000/month pushes you into a meaningfully higher tax bracket than either individual salary alone would suggest — creating a real gap between what was withheld and what you actually owe.

Why this becomes a problem at tax return time

SARS assesses your true total tax liability, from all income sources combined, when you file your annual return — at which point the shortfall from under-withholding throughout the year becomes visible and payable, often as an unexpected, sometimes substantial, lump sum owed.

Practical ways to avoid an unpleasant surprise

  • Request additional voluntary PAYE deduction from one employer to cover the anticipated shortfall
  • Apply for a SARS tax directive, which instructs employers on the correct combined tax treatment
  • Set aside funds independently throughout the year specifically to cover the anticipated year-end liability
  • File early and proactively to understand your position rather than discovering it at the deadline

When a second income is genuinely worth the added complexity

The tax complexity of a second income stream should not overshadow its real value — additional income remains additional income even after accounting for a correctly calculated tax liability. Why two employers rarely withhold the correct combined tax

Each employer calculates PAYE as though their salary is your only source of income, applying the tax tables and rebate independently. When you have two jobs, this means the primary rebate and lower tax brackets are effectively applied twice — once by each employer — which results in less total tax being withheld across both jobs than you actually owe on your combined annual income.

A worked example of the shortfall

Suppose you earn R15,000/month from a primary job and R8,000/month from a second role. Each employer taxes their portion as though it were your full income, likely applying a low or even zero effective rate to the smaller second income. But combined, your true annual income of R276,000 sits in a higher bracket than either job alone suggests, creating a real gap between what was withheld and what is actually owed.

How to avoid an unpleasant surprise at tax time

  • Apply for a SARS tax directive — allows one employer (typically the smaller income source) to withhold tax at a specified higher rate to better match your true combined liability
  • Register as provisional taxpayer — if the shortfall is substantial, this formalises the process of paying the difference proactively rather than as a surprise year-end bill
  • Set aside funds voluntarily — even without formal provisions, proactively saving an estimated shortfall amount each month prevents a cash flow crisis when your return is assessed

Declaring both income sources correctly

Both IRP5 certificates must be declared on your annual tax return — SARS receives both directly from your employers regardless, so omitting one is not a viable strategy and will be flagged during assessment. The return calculation reconciles your true combined tax liability against what was actually withheld across both jobs.

When a second income is genuinely worth the tax complexity

Despite the additional tax owed at reconciliation, a second income source still results in more total after-tax money than not having it — the shortfall represents tax you always owed, simply deferred rather than newly created. The practical lesson is planning for it, not avoiding second income opportunities out of tax concern.

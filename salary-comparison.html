Why comparing offers on gross salary alone is misleading

Two job offers with identical gross salaries can leave you with meaningfully different take-home pay once medical aid contributions, retirement fund structures, travel allowances, and other deductions are factored in. Comparing offers on gross salary alone is one of the most common — and costly — mistakes in a job search or negotiation.

A R600,000 salary with no benefits and mandatory 15% retirement contribution gives you very different actual value than a R550,000 salary with generous medical aid subsidy, 7.5% retirement contribution, and a guaranteed 13th cheque. The lower-sounding offer might actually put more cash in your pocket each month while building better long-term wealth.

The comparison trap: Recruiters often quote CTC (Cost to Company) figures that include employer retirement contributions, medical aid subsidies, and other benefits you never actually see in your bank account. Always ask for the breakdown and calculate your actual monthly take-home before comparing offers.

CTC vs gross salary: understanding the difference

South African employers typically quote offers in one of two ways, and the difference can be R50,000-R150,000 per year:

Gross salary

Your cash compensation before any deductions. This is what appears on your payslip before tax, UIF, retirement contributions, and medical aid are subtracted. If an offer is R600,000 gross, that's your starting point for calculating take-home.

CTC (Cost to Company)

Everything the employer spends on you annually, including:

  • Gross salary
  • Employer retirement/pension fund contributions
  • Employer medical aid subsidies
  • UIF contributions (1% from employer)
  • SDL (Skills Development Levy) — 1% on salaries over R500,000
  • Any other benefits (group life, disability cover, etc.)

Worked example: Same role, different structures

Component Offer A (R700k CTC) Offer B (R650k Gross)
Base salary R580,000 R650,000
Employer retirement (15%) R87,000 R0
Employer medical aid subsidy R24,000 R0
UIF (employer portion) R8,712 R8,712
SDL R5,800 R6,500
Total CTC R705,512 R665,212
Your monthly take-home R32,500 R36,800
Retirement savings building R7,250/month Depends on your contribution

The insight: Offer A has higher CTC but lower monthly take-home because of the large employer retirement contribution. Offer B gives you more cash now but no employer retirement support. Which is better depends on your priorities: current cash flow vs forced retirement savings.

Travel allowance tax: the 80% rule

Travel allowances are taxed differently from regular salary in South Africa, and understanding the rules can save you thousands in tax:

Default treatment: 80% taxable

By default, SARS assumes you use your vehicle 80% for private purposes and 20% for business. Therefore:

  • 80% of travel allowance is included in your taxable income
  • 20% is tax-free (assumed business use)

Example: R120,000 annual travel allowance

Scenario Taxable Portion Tax-Free Portion Tax Impact (30% bracket)
Default (80% business use not proven) R96,000 R24,000 R28,800 additional tax
With logbook (>80% business use) R24,000 R96,000 R7,200 tax (saving R21,600)
With logbook (<80% business use) R120,000 (100%) R0 R36,000 tax
Keep a logbook: If you receive a travel allowance and use your vehicle more than 80% for business, keeping a proper logbook can save you R20,000+ in tax annually. The logbook must record date, destination, purpose, and kilometres for each business trip.

Fixed cost vs actual cost method

When claiming travel deductions against your allowance, you can choose:

  • Fixed cost method: Use SARS deemed rates per kilometre (simpler, often better for low business km)
  • Actual cost method: Track all vehicle expenses (fuel, maintenance, insurance, finance costs) and claim business portion (better for high business km)

Bonuses, 13th cheques, and performance incentives

Not all bonuses are created equal. Understanding the structure is crucial for accurate comparison:

Guaranteed 13th cheque

A contractual obligation — the employer must pay it regardless of performance or company results. This is effectively part of your guaranteed annual compensation and should be included in comparisons.

Example: R500,000 salary + guaranteed R50,000 13th cheque = R550,000 guaranteed annual income.

Discretionary performance bonus

Paid based on individual and/or company performance. May be:

  • Target-based: "Up to 20% of salary if targets met" — not guaranteed
  • Discretionary: Management decides annually whether to pay bonuses at all
  • Formula-based: Tied to specific metrics (revenue, profit, etc.)

Comparison approach: For discretionary bonuses, either exclude them entirely (conservative) or include at 50-70% of target value (realistic expectation). Never count 100% of a discretionary bonus in your financial planning.

Sign-on bonuses

One-time payments when you join. These don't recur, so divide by the expected tenure to annualise:

  • R50,000 sign-on bonus, planning to stay 3 years = R16,667/year equivalent
  • R100,000 sign-on, staying 2 years = R50,000/year equivalent

How bonuses are taxed

Bonuses are taxed at your marginal rate in the month received, which can push you into a higher bracket temporarily. A R50,000 bonus for someone in the 36% bracket results in approximately R32,000 after tax — not the full R50,000.

Bonus Amount Marginal Tax Rate Tax on Bonus After-Tax Bonus
R20,000 26% R5,200 R14,800
R50,000 36% R18,000 R32,000
R100,000 39% R39,000 R61,000
R200,000 41% R82,000 R118,000

Retirement fund contributions: the long-term value

Employer retirement contributions don't appear in your monthly take-home, but they represent real value building for your future. Two offers with different retirement structures require careful comparison:

How employer contributions work

  • Defined contribution: Employer contributes a % of salary (typically 7.5-15%) to your pension/provident fund
  • Tax treatment: Employer contributions are tax-deductible for the company and not taxed as a fringe benefit to you (up to limits)
  • Your access: Locked until retirement age (55+) or resignation/change of job
  • Compound growth: Grows tax-free until withdrawal at retirement

Comparing retirement structures

Scenario Employer Contribution Annual Value 20-Year Value (10% growth) Monthly Take-Home Impact
Offer A: No employer contribution 0% R0 R0 Higher (no deduction)
Offer B: 7.5% employer contribution 7.5% R45,000 R2.6 million Same (employer pays)
Offer C: 15% employer contribution 15% R90,000 R5.2 million Same (employer pays)

The insight: A 15% employer retirement contribution on R600,000 salary adds R90,000/year (R5.2 million over 20 years) to your retirement savings — at no cost to your take-home pay. This is enormous long-term value that doesn't show up in monthly comparisons.

Your own retirement contributions

Your personal retirement contributions (RA or pension) reduce your take-home but also reduce your tax:

  • Deductible up to 27.5% of taxable income (capped at R350,000/year)
  • At 36% marginal rate, R100,000 contribution costs you R64,000 after tax savings
  • Effective "cost" depends on your tax bracket

Employer medical aid subsidies

Some employers contribute toward your medical aid premium:

  • Fixed amount: Employer pays R2,000/month toward your medical aid
  • Percentage: Employer pays 50-75% of your premium
  • Family vs single: Some employers only subsidise main member, others include family

Tax treatment: Employer medical aid subsidies are taxed as a fringe benefit — added to your taxable income. However, you still receive the medical aid tax credits, which offset some of the tax impact.

Beyond the numbers: what a calculator cannot capture

Take-home pay is the clearest, most comparable number between two offers, but it's not the only factor that determines which job actually leaves you better off. Consider these non-financial factors:

Career growth trajectory

  • Promotion timeline: How quickly do people advance? What's typical time to next level?
  • Skill development: Training budgets, conference attendance, certification support
  • Mentorship: Access to senior leaders and learning opportunities
  • Industry position: Is this a leading company that enhances your CV?

Work-life balance

  • Hours: Expected working hours, overtime culture, weekend work
  • Flexibility: Remote work options, flexible hours, compressed work weeks
  • Leave: Annual leave days (15-25 typical), sick leave, family responsibility leave
  • Travel requirements: How much travel is expected? Is it compensated?

Job security and company stability

  • Financial health: Is the company profitable and growing?
  • Industry trends: Is this a growing or declining industry?
  • Turnover rates: Do people stay long-term or leave quickly?
  • Recent changes: Layoffs, restructuring, acquisitions

Location and commute

  • Commute time: An extra hour daily = 250 hours/year lost
  • Commute cost: Fuel, vehicle wear, or public transport costs
  • Relocation: Would you need to move? What's the cost of living difference?
  • Quality of life: City amenities, schools, healthcare, safety

Company culture and values

  • Management style: Supportive vs micromanaging
  • Team dynamics: Collaborative vs competitive
  • Values alignment: Does the company's mission resonate with you?
  • Diversity and inclusion: Do you see people like yourself represented?
The 5-year test: Ask yourself: "In 5 years, which position will have taught me more, given me better opportunities, and left me happier?" Sometimes the lower-paying offer wins this test decisively — and that's a valid reason to choose it.

Negotiating with comparison data in hand

Once you know the real take-home gap between two offers, you're in a stronger position to negotiate. Here's how to use this information effectively:

Scenario 1: You prefer Offer B but Offer A pays more

Your approach: "I'm very excited about the opportunity at [Company B] and believe I can add significant value. However, I have another offer that provides R3,000 more per month after tax. Is there any flexibility to close this gap, either through base salary adjustment, sign-on bonus, or enhanced benefits?"

Possible outcomes:

  • They match or come close to the competing offer
  • They offer a sign-on bonus to bridge the gap
  • They commit to a salary review at 6 months
  • They explain why they can't match but emphasise other benefits

Scenario 2: You have Offer A and want to improve it

Your approach: "I'm very interested in joining the team. Based on my research and the value I'll bring, I was hoping we could discuss the compensation package. Would there be flexibility to adjust [specific component] to better align with market rates?"

Negotiable components:

  • Base salary (most common)
  • Sign-on bonus (one-time, easier for companies to approve)
  • Performance bonus target or guarantee
  • Additional annual leave days
  • Flexible work arrangements
  • Professional development budget
  • Earlier performance review (6 months vs 12)

What not to do

  • Don't bluff: Only mention competing offers you actually have
  • Don't make it personal: Keep negotiations professional and value-focused
  • Don't accept immediately: Always ask for 24-48 hours to consider
  • Don't focus only on salary: Consider the total package
  • Don't burn bridges: Even if you decline, remain professional

Your offer comparison checklist

Before accepting either offer, ensure you have clarity on all these points:

Compensation structure

  1. Is the quoted figure gross salary or CTC?
  2. What's included in CTC (list all components)?
  3. What's the monthly take-home after all deductions?
  4. Is there a guaranteed 13th cheque or bonus?
  5. What's the typical annual increase percentage?
  6. When is the first performance review?

Benefits

  1. Medical aid: Is it compulsory? What's the employer subsidy?
  2. Retirement fund: What % does employer contribute? Can you choose the fund?
  3. Group life and disability cover: What's included?
  4. Travel allowance: Is there one? What's the tax treatment?
  5. Other benefits: Cell phone, laptop, gym membership, etc.

Leave and work arrangements

  1. Annual leave: How many days?
  2. Sick leave, family responsibility leave: What's the policy?
  3. Flexible work: Is remote or hybrid work available?
  4. Working hours: What are the expectations?
  5. Overtime: Is it paid or time off in lieu?

Career development

  1. Training and development budget
  2. Conference and certification support
  3. Typical promotion timeline
  4. Mentorship opportunities
  5. Internal mobility options

Contract details

  1. Is this permanent or fixed-term?
  2. Probation period: How long?
  3. Notice period: What's required from both sides?
  4. Restraint of trade: Is there one? What are the terms?
  5. Intellectual property: Who owns what you create?

Common offer comparison mistakes

  • Comparing gross instead of take-home: A R600,000 gross salary with high mandatory deductions may give less take-home than R550,000 with lower deductions
  • Ignoring employer retirement contributions: These don't show in monthly pay but build enormous long-term value
  • Counting discretionary bonuses as guaranteed: Only include bonuses that are contractually guaranteed or highly likely
  • Forgetting about travel allowance tax: 80% of travel allowance is taxable by default — this can add R20,000+ to your annual tax
  • Not considering medical aid subsidies: A R2,000/month subsidy saves you R24,000/year — equivalent to a R35,000 salary increase before tax
  • Ignoring sign-on bonuses: These are one-time but can bridge a compensation gap significantly
  • Not reading the fine print: Restraints of trade, notice periods, and other clauses have real financial implications
  • Focusing only on year one: Consider the 3-5 year trajectory, not just starting salary
  • Not getting it in writing: Verbal promises about bonuses, increases, or benefits mean nothing — get everything in the written contract
  • Making emotional decisions: Use data to inform your decision, even if you ultimately choose based on gut feeling

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I compare two job offers in South Africa?

Compare the after-tax take-home pay, not just gross salary. Factor in medical aid contributions, retirement fund structures, travel allowances (80% taxable), bonus guarantees, and employer benefits. Two offers with identical gross salaries can differ by R2,000-R5,000/month in actual take-home depending on how benefits are structured.

What is the difference between CTC and gross salary?

CTC (Cost to Company) includes everything the employer spends on you: gross salary, employer retirement contributions, medical aid subsidies, UIF, SDL, and other benefits. Gross salary is just your cash compensation before deductions. Always ask whether an offer is quoted as CTC or gross — the difference can be R50,000-R150,000/year.

How is a travel allowance taxed in South Africa?

By default, 80% of a travel allowance is included in taxable income (20% is tax-free for business use). If you use your vehicle more than 80% for business (with a logbook), only 20% is taxable. If less than 80% business use, 100% may be taxable. Always keep a logbook to optimise your travel allowance tax treatment.

Is a 13th cheque better than a higher monthly salary?

A guaranteed 13th cheque provides the same annual income as a 8.33% higher monthly salary, but timing differs. A 13th cheque gives you a lump sum (good for large expenses or savings), while higher monthly salary provides better cash flow. Both are taxed the same annually. A discretionary bonus is riskier — only count it if contractually guaranteed.

How do employer retirement contributions affect my take-home?

Employer contributions to your pension/provident fund reduce your immediate take-home but build retirement savings and reduce your tax. Contributions up to 27.5% of income (capped at R350,000/year) are tax-deductible. An employer contributing 15% vs 7.5% adds significant long-term value, even if monthly take-home is lower.

Should I choose higher salary or better benefits?

Calculate total value including benefits. A R500,000 salary with no benefits may give less total value than R450,000 with generous medical aid subsidy, 15% employer retirement contribution, and guaranteed bonus. Use a comparison calculator to model both scenarios. Also consider non-financial benefits: flexible work, growth opportunities, job security.

How much medical aid tax credit do I get in South Africa?

Medical aid tax credits (2026/2027): R376/month for main member, R376/month for first dependant, R254/month for each additional dependant. A family of four (member + spouse + 2 children) gets R1,260/month in tax credits (R15,120/year). These reduce your tax payable directly, making medical aid more affordable.

What questions should I ask before accepting a job offer?

Ask: Is the salary gross or CTC? What percentage does employer contribute to retirement fund? Is medical aid subsidised and by how much? Is the bonus guaranteed or discretionary? How many leave days? Is there flexible/remote work? What's the typical annual increase? What's the notice period? Request the full offer in writing with all benefits detailed.

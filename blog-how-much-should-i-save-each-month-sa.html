Where your savings should go, in order

  • 1. Emergency fund — 3–6 months of expenses first
  • 2. High-interest debt — clear it; the interest saved is a guaranteed return
  • 3. Retirement — a retirement annuity also cuts your tax
  • 4. Other goals — house deposit, investments, a tax-free savings account

Make the number real

The honest starting benchmark: 15% of income

Financial planners commonly cite 15% of gross income, including any retirement contributions, as a reasonable target savings rate for most working South Africans. This is a starting benchmark rather than a universal rule — those starting later in their career, or pursuing early financial independence, typically need to save meaningfully more.

Adjusting the benchmark for your actual situation

SituationSuggested savings rate
Early career, just starting out10% (focus on building the habit)
On track, standard timeline15%
Starting later or catching up20-25%
Pursuing early financial independence (FIRE)30-50%+

Where each rand of saving should go, in priority order

  • First, a starter emergency fund — even R10,000-R15,000 protects against small financial shocks
  • Then, eliminate high-interest debt — the guaranteed "return" from clearing 20%+ interest debt outperforms most investments
  • Then, maximise retirement contributions — particularly valuable given the immediate tax deduction available
  • Then, additional goals — house deposit, further investments, or other specific targets

Why consistency outweighs the exact percentage

A consistently maintained 12% savings rate over many years will typically outperform an inconsistent attempt at 20% that collapses after a few months. Building an automated, sustainable habit matters more than chasing an ambitious number that proves unsustainable.

The 15% baseline, and why it is a floor, not a ceiling

Most financial planners in South Africa converge on 15% of gross income as a reasonable minimum savings rate, encompassing both retirement contributions and other savings goals combined. This figure is not arbitrary — it derives from working backwards from typical retirement income needs and reasonable investment growth assumptions over a standard working career.

Adjusting the target for your specific starting point

SituationSuggested savings rate
Starting your career, minimal debt10-15% — build the habit first
Mid-career, on track15-20% — the standard sustained target
Starting later, catching up20-30% — meaningfully higher to compensate for lost time
Pursuing early financial independence30-50%+ — an aggressive, deliberate strategy

Where the first portion of your saving should always go

Regardless of your target percentage, the sequencing matters: build a starter emergency fund first (even a modest R10,000-R15,000 buffer), then prioritise clearing any high-interest debt, and only then direct the remaining savings capacity toward retirement and investment goals. Saving aggressively while carrying 25% credit card debt is mathematically counterproductive.

Making the percentage concrete

An abstract 15% target becomes real once translated into rands. On a R25,000 monthly income, 15% is R3,750 — a specific, budgetable number that can be automated as a debit order the day after payday, removing the monthly decision entirely and protecting the habit from any given month's spending pressure.

The compounding reward for consistency

A practical starting rule if you are unsure

If 15% feels unreachable right now, start at whatever percentage you can sustain — even 5% — and increase it by one percentage point every time you get a raise, until you reach a comfortable target. Small, consistent increases compound into meaningful savings rates over a few years without requiring a dramatic lifestyle change all at once.

The 15% benchmark, and where it comes from

Financial planners commonly recommend saving at least 15% of gross income, a figure derived from modelling what most people need to accumulate over a typical working career to retire comfortably at a reasonable age. It is not an arbitrary round number — it reflects the math of compound growth applied over several decades of a standard career length.

What counts toward your 15%

This figure should include all forms of saving and investing combined: retirement annuity contributions, employer pension fund contributions (yours and your employer's matched portion), tax-free savings account contributions, and any other investment contributions. It is a total savings rate, not solely what leaves your take-home pay directly.

The order in which your savings should flow

  • 1. Emergency fund first — 3-6 months of expenses provides the foundation everything else depends on
  • 2. High-interest debt second — clearing debt above roughly 15% interest is a guaranteed "return" that beats most investments
  • 3. Retirement contributions third — particularly up to any employer matching, which is effectively free money
  • 4. Other goals fourth — house deposit, further investing, or other specific targets

Making 15% feel achievable, not abstract

The 15-20% guideline, and where it comes from

Financial planners commonly recommend saving 15-20% of gross income, a figure derived from modelling what most people need to accumulate over a typical working career to retire comfortably, while also building shorter-term savings for emergencies and major purchases along the way. It is a target to work toward, not necessarily where you need to start immediately.

A realistic starting point if 15-20% feels out of reach

15%+You are at or above the standard guideline

Why the percentage matters more than the rand amount

A percentage-based target scales naturally with your income, which is why it is generally more useful than a fixed rand figure. As your salary grows through raises and promotions, maintaining the same percentage means your actual saved amount grows proportionally, without needing to consciously recalculate a target each time your income changes.

The order in which savings should be allocated

  • 1. Emergency fund first — 3-6 months of expenses provides the foundation everything else depends on
  • 2. High-interest debt second — clearing debt above roughly 15% interest is effectively a guaranteed return
  • 3. Retirement contributions third — especially valuable if there is an employer match or meaningful tax deduction available
  • 4. Other specific goals fourth — a house deposit, further education, or other defined objectives

What a consistent savings rate actually becomes over time

Saving 15% of a R25,000 salary means R3,750 monthly. Invested consistently at a 10% average annual return for 20 years, that contribution alone — without accounting for any starting balance — grows to well over R2.5 million, illustrating why the percentage habit matters more than any single month's amount.

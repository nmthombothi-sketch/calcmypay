Understanding every rand of your salary

Between PAYE, UIF, medical aid credits, retirement contributions, and the occasional bonus or overtime payment, very few South Africans have a completely clear picture of how their gross salary becomes their actual take-home pay. These seven tools break down every part of that journey, using the latest SARS 2027 tax tables and BCEA regulations.

Your payslip is more complex than it appears. A R40,000 gross monthly salary might result in anywhere from R28,000 to R34,000 take-home depending on your specific deductions, medical aid structure, retirement contributions, and whether you're paying medical aid out of pocket or receiving employer subsidy. Understanding these differences is essential for budgeting, negotiating, and financial planning.

Common salary deductions explained

Statutory deductions (mandatory)

PAYE (Pay As You Earn)

Income tax deducted monthly by your employer and paid to SARS. Calculated using the tax brackets above, less any rebates and medical aid tax credits you're entitled to. Your employer must provide an IRP5 certificate annually showing total tax paid.

UIF (Unemployment Insurance Fund)

1% of gross salary deducted from employee, plus 1% contributed by employer (2% total). Capped at R177.12/month based on maximum earnings of R17,712/month. Provides benefits for unemployment, maternity, illness, and dependants of deceased contributors.

SDL (Skills Development Levy)

1% of total salaries paid by employers with payroll over R500,000/year. Not deducted from your salary — employer pays this directly to SARS. Funds SETA training programs.

Voluntary deductions (chosen by you or in your contract)

Medical aid contributions

Monthly premiums to your medical aid scheme. You receive tax credits of R376/month for main member, R376/month for first dependant, and R254/month for each additional dependant. Some employers subsidise part or all of your medical aid.

Retirement fund contributions

Contributions to pension, provident, or retirement annuity funds. Tax-deductible up to 27.5% of taxable income (capped at R350,000/year). Many employers offer matching contributions — always take full employer match as it's free money.

Group life and disability insurance

Often offered through employer schemes at group rates. May be fully employer-paid, shared cost, or employee-paid. Provides death and disability cover, typically 2-4x annual salary.

Garnishee orders (emolument attachment orders)

Court-ordered deductions for debt repayment. Legally limited to 25% of your salary. Must be issued by a court — employers cannot deduct for debt without a court order.

Allowances and their tax treatment

Travel allowance

By default, 80% is taxable (20% assumed business use). If you keep a logbook showing more than 80% business use, only 20% is taxable. If less than 80% business use, 100% may be taxable. You can claim actual vehicle expenses against the taxable portion.

Subsistence allowance

For meals and incidental costs when travelling for work. SARS sets deemed rates (approximately R485/day for meals in South Africa). Amounts within deemed rates are tax-free; excess is taxable.

Cell phone and laptop allowances

Generally taxable as a fringe benefit unless strictly for business use and you can prove this. Many employers include a taxable "communication allowance" in CTC.

Basic Conditions of Employment Act (BCEA) essentials

The BCEA sets minimum employment conditions in South Africa. Key provisions affecting your pay:

Working hours

  • Maximum: 45 hours per week
  • 5-day week: Maximum 9 hours per day
  • 6-day week: Maximum 8 hours per day
  • Overtime: Maximum 3 hours per day, 10 hours per week

Overtime pay rates

  • Monday-Saturday overtime: 1.5× normal hourly rate
  • Sunday work: 2× normal hourly rate (or time off in lieu)
  • Public holidays: 2× normal rate if worked, or paid day off if not worked

BCEA earnings threshold (2026)

Employees earning above R254,478.46 per year (R21,206.54/month) are not automatically entitled to overtime pay under the BCEA. However, your contract may still provide for overtime. Below this threshold, overtime provisions apply automatically.

Leave entitlements

  • Annual leave: 21 consecutive days (15 working days) per year
  • Sick leave: 30 days in a 36-month cycle (or 36 days for 6-day week workers)
  • Family responsibility leave: 3 days per year (for employees with 4+ months service)
  • Maternity leave: 4 months unpaid (can claim from UIF)

Salary negotiation: using these tools strategically

These calculators become powerful negotiation tools when used strategically:

Before negotiating a raise

  1. Calculate your current true take-home: Use the salary calculator with your exact deductions
  2. Research market rates: Use our salary database to find comparable roles
  3. Model your target salary: Use the calculator to see the after-tax impact of your target
  4. Calculate the gap: Know exactly how much more per month you're asking for
  5. Prepare your case: Document achievements, additional responsibilities, market data

Evaluating a job offer

  1. Get complete CTC breakdown: Ask for all components, not just headline number
  2. Model both offers: Use the salary comparison tool for side-by-side
  3. Include all benefits: Medical aid subsidies, retirement contributions, bonuses
  4. Calculate true take-home: Not just gross — compare what lands in your account
  5. Factor in non-financial: Growth opportunities, work-life balance, commute

Common negotiation mistakes

  • Comparing gross not net: Two R600k offers can differ by R3,000/month after tax depending on structure
  • Ignoring employer benefits: A 15% employer retirement contribution is R90,000/year of hidden value
  • Forgetting cost of living: R500k in Cape Town ≠ R500k in Bloemfontein
  • Not getting it in writing: Verbal promises mean nothing — everything must be in the contract
  • Negotiating only salary: Consider signing bonus, additional leave, flexible work, development budget
The reverse calculation trick: When someone asks "what's your current salary?", give them your take-home pay and ask them to work backwards. This forces them to show you their full package structure rather than just the gross number — and prevents lowball offers that sound high in gross but are actually less after tax and benefits.

Optimising your salary for tax efficiency

How your salary is structured can significantly affect your after-tax income. Common tax-efficient strategies:

Maximise retirement contributions

Contributions up to 27.5% of taxable income (R350,000 cap) are tax-deductible. At a 36% marginal rate, R100,000 in retirement contributions costs you only R64,000 after tax savings — effectively a 56% instant return on investment.

Use a travel allowance strategically

If you have significant business travel, a travel allowance with a logbook showing >80% business use can be more tax-efficient than a higher salary. Only 20% of the allowance is taxable versus 100% of salary.

Consider salary sacrifice arrangements

Some benefits can be provided through salary sacrifice (like additional retirement contributions or medical aid top-ups) which may be more tax-efficient than taking the cash and paying for these yourself.

Time your bonus wisely

If you have flexibility on when to receive a bonus, taking it in a lower-income year (perhaps when taking a sabbatical or between contracts) can reduce the marginal tax rate applied.

Split income with spouse where legal

If you own a business or have investment income, legitimate income splitting with a lower-earning spouse can reduce overall household tax burden. This must be genuine — SARS cracks down on artificial arrangements.

Warning: Never engage in tax evasion or aggressive avoidance schemes. SARS has sophisticated detection systems and penalties are severe (up to 200% of tax avoided plus criminal prosecution). Stick to legitimate tax planning strategies.

Reading your payslip: line by line

Understanding every line on your payslip helps you verify accuracy and spot errors:

Earnings section

  • Basic salary: Your contractual monthly salary before allowances
  • Allowances: Travel, cell phone, housing, etc. — each listed separately
  • Overtime: Hours worked and rate applied (1.5x or 2x)
  • Bonus: Any bonus payments in the month
  • Gross pay: Total earnings before deductions

Deductions section

  • PAYE: Income tax withheld — should match calculator outputs
  • UIF: Should be 1% of gross (max R177.12)
  • Medical aid: Your contribution (employer subsidy shown separately if applicable)
  • Retirement fund: Your contribution to pension/provident/RA
  • Other deductions: Union fees, insurance, loans, garnishee orders

Employer contributions (often shown separately)

  • Employer UIF: 1% of your salary (you don't pay this)
  • Employer retirement: Employer's contribution to your fund
  • Medical aid subsidy: Amount employer pays toward your medical aid
  • SDL: Skills Development Levy (1% if payroll >R500k)
  • COIDA: Workmen's compensation insurance

Net pay

Gross earnings minus all deductions = your actual take-home pay. This is the amount deposited into your bank account.

Year-end tax considerations

Beyond monthly calculations, consider these annual factors:

IRP5 certificate

Your employer must issue an IRP5 by May each year showing total earnings and tax deducted for the previous tax year. Check this carefully against your payslips — errors can cause SARS assessment problems.

Additional deductions to claim

When filing your annual tax return, you may claim additional deductions not captured monthly:

  • Travel expenses: Against your travel allowance using logbook
  • Home office: If you have a dedicated home office (strict requirements)
  • Wear and tear: On equipment you purchased for work
  • Donations: To approved PBOs (Section 18A certificates required)
  • Medical expenses: Out-of-pocket amounts not covered by medical aid

Provisional tax

If you have income not subject to PAYE (rental income, freelance work, investment income over certain thresholds), you may need to register as a provisional taxpayer and pay tax twice yearly (August and February).

Common salary calculation questions

Why does my take-home vary month to month?

Common reasons for variation: overtime payments, bonuses, commission, February having fewer days (affects daily rate calculations), tax directive changes, or changes in your deductions (like medical aid dependants).

What's the difference between CTC, gross, and net?

  • CTC (Cost to Company): Everything the employer spends on you — gross salary + employer benefits
  • Gross salary: Your cash earnings before any deductions
  • Net salary: Your actual take-home pay after all deductions

How do I check if my PAYE is correct?

Use our PAYE calculator with your exact gross income and deductions. Compare the result to your payslip. Small differences (R50-R100) may be due to rounding or timing, but large discrepancies should be queried with your payroll department.

What if my employer is deducting too much tax?

First verify with the calculator. If the calculator confirms overpayment, approach payroll with your calculations. They may have applied an incorrect tax directive or missed your tax credits. Any overpaid tax will be refunded by SARS when you file your annual return.

Can I change my tax deductions?

You can request a tax directive from SARS if your standard PAYE calculation doesn't reflect your situation (e.g., you have significant deductions not captured by your employer). SARS will issue a directive to your employer specifying the correct tax to withhold.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate my take-home pay in South Africa?

Start with gross annual salary, subtract PAYE income tax (using SARS 2027 brackets), UIF (1% capped at R177.12/month), retirement fund contributions, medical aid contributions, and other deductions. Most South Africans take home 65-80% of gross salary depending on their tax bracket and deductions.

What are the SARS tax brackets for 2026/2027?

SARS 2027 brackets: R0-R245,100 (18%), R245,101-R383,100 (26%), R383,101-R530,200 (31%), R530,201-R695,800 (36%), R695,801-R887,000 (39%), R887,001-R1,878,600 (41%), above R1,878,600 (45%). Tax rebates: R17,820 (under 65), R27,720 (65-74), R37,020 (75+).

How is UIF calculated in South Africa?

UIF is 1% of gross salary from employee plus 1% from employer (2% total). Employee contribution is capped at R177.12/month (R2,125.44/year) based on maximum earnings threshold of R17,712/month.

How is overtime calculated?

Under BCEA: 1.5× normal rate for overtime Monday-Saturday, 2× rate for Sunday and public holidays. Employees earning above R254,478.46/year aren't automatically entitled to overtime unless their contract specifies it.

How is a bonus taxed?

Bonuses are taxed at your marginal rate. A R50,000 bonus for someone in the 36% bracket results in approximately R18,000 tax (R32,000 after tax). Some employers use the annualisation method which can temporarily push you into a higher bracket.

What's the difference between gross and net salary?

Gross is total earnings before deductions (tax, UIF, retirement, medical aid). Net is what actually lands in your bank account. CTC includes gross plus employer contributions to benefits.

How much tax on R500,000 salary?

On R500,000 (2027), PAYE would be approximately R114,743 before rebates. After R17,820 primary rebate, you'd pay about R96,923 in tax — effective rate 19.4%. Add UIF R1,771.20 for total deductions of approximately R98,694.

What is the minimum wage in 2026?

National minimum wage is R28.79 per ordinary hour. For a 45-hour week, this equals approximately R5,615/month. Farm and domestic workers have the same minimum wage since 2022.

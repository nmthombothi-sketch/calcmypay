Why net worth is the single best financial health metric

Income tells you what you earn. Net worth tells you what you have actually built. Two people earning identical salaries can have wildly different net worth depending on their spending, saving, and debt habits — which is why financial advisers treat net worth, not income, as the real scoreboard of financial progress.

A high-income earner who spends everything and carries substantial debt may have negative net worth, while a moderate earner who saves consistently may have built millions. Net worth captures the cumulative effect of every financial decision you've made — the good and the bad.

The wealth vs income distinction: Income is what flows in this month. Wealth is what remains after years of decisions. Focusing on income alone is like judging a business by its revenue while ignoring its balance sheet. Net worth is your personal balance sheet.

What actually counts as an asset

Not everything you own should be counted at full value in a genuine net worth calculation. Understanding what to include — and at what value — is essential for an honest financial picture.

Count fully at current value

  • Cash and savings: Bank accounts, money market funds, fixed deposits
  • Stokvel savings: Your current balance in any stokvel (savings, grocery, burial)
  • Investments: Unit trusts, ETFs, shares, bonds
  • Retirement savings: RA balance, pension fund value, provident fund value, preservation funds
  • Tax-Free Savings Account: Full current balance
  • Property: Current market value (not purchase price or municipal valuation)

Count conservatively

  • Vehicles: Current trade-in or private sale value, not purchase price. Cars depreciate 15-20% annually in SA. Use TransUnion Auto Value or WeBuyCars for realistic estimates.
  • Business value: Only if you have a realistic valuation or recent offer. Many small businesses are worth less than owners believe — be conservative.
  • Household goods: Generally exclude unless genuinely valuable (art, antiques). Everyday furniture and appliances depreciate rapidly and aren't liquid.

Generally exclude

  • Future inheritance: Until actually received, it's not yours
  • Expected bonuses or salary increases: Until in your bank account
  • Lottery tickets or speculative bets: Treat as entertainment expense, not assets
  • Money owed to you: Unless legally enforceable and likely to be paid

South African-specific assets to include

Retirement Annuities (RA)

Your RA balance is a real asset, even though it's inaccessible until age 55. Include the full current value shown on your latest statement. RAs offer significant tax benefits — contributions are deductible up to 27.5% of income (R350,000/year maximum), making them one of the most powerful wealth-building tools available to South Africans.

Pension and Provident Funds

If you're employed with a company pension or provident fund, check your latest benefit statement for the current fund value. This is often the largest asset for middle-income South Africans — many people have R500,000-R2 million in their pension fund without realising it.

Preservation Funds

If you've changed jobs and preserved your retirement savings, these funds remain yours and should be included in your net worth. Many South Africans forget about preservation funds from previous employers — track them down and include them.

Tax-Free Savings Accounts (TFSA)

TFSAs allow you to invest R40,000/year (R500,000 lifetime) with all growth and withdrawals completely tax-free. Include your full TFSA balance — these are powerful long-term wealth-building vehicles, especially for younger investors.

Stokvel Savings

Stokvels are legitimate savings vehicles used by millions of South Africans. Whether it's a December savings stokvel, grocery stokvel, or burial society, your current balance is a real asset. Include it in your cash and savings category.

The debt side is where honesty matters most

It is tempting to understate debt or forget smaller balances — a store card, an outstanding medical bill, money owed to family. A genuinely useful net worth figure requires including everything you owe, since the whole point of the exercise is an honest financial position, not a flattering one.

Include all debts

  • Home loan/bond: Current outstanding balance from your latest statement
  • Car finance: Current settlement value (not original loan amount)
  • Student loans: NSFAS or private student loan balances
  • Credit cards: Full outstanding balance on all cards
  • Personal loans: All personal loan balances
  • Store accounts: Retail credit balances (Edgars, Truworths, etc.)
  • Buy-now-pay-later: Payflex, PayJustNow, and similar services
  • Family loans: Money borrowed from family members — include it even if informal
  • Medical debt: Outstanding medical bills not yet paid
  • Tax debt: Any amounts owed to SARS
The hidden debt trap: Many South Africans have R20,000-R50,000 in "forgotten" debt — store accounts they don't use, small personal loans, old medical bills. These don't disappear; they accumulate interest and damage your credit score. Pull your free annual credit report from TransUnion, Experian, or XDS to see the full picture.

South African net worth benchmarks by age

While individual circumstances vary enormously, having a benchmark helps you understand where you stand relative to peers. These figures represent approximate middle-class South African net worth by age group in 2026:

Age Group Typical Range Common Situation
Under 25 -R100k to R50k Student debt, early career, minimal savings
25-34 R50k to R500k Building career, possibly first property, some retirement savings
35-44 R500k to R2 million Established career, growing property equity, meaningful retirement savings
45-54 R2 million to R5 million Peak earning years, substantial property equity, significant retirement savings
55-64 R3 million to R8 million Nearing retirement, paid-off or low-bond property, large retirement funds
65+ R2 million to R10 million+ Retired, drawing down savings, property often primary asset

A more personalised benchmark

A useful rule of thumb from US wealth research (adaptable to SA) suggests your target net worth at any age should be approximately:

Target = (Age × Annual Income) ÷ 10

So at age 40 earning R500,000/year, a target net worth would be around R2 million. At age 30 earning R300,000/year, around R900,000. This isn't a hard rule but a directional guide.

Context matters enormously: A 35-year-old with R2 million net worth in a paid-off R3 million property is in a very different position from one with R2 million in volatile crypto. Consider not just the number but the composition and liquidity of your assets.

Reading your debt-to-asset ratio correctly

Your debt-to-asset ratio (total debts ÷ total assets × 100) reveals how much of what you "own" is actually financed by debt. This is often more informative than net worth alone:

Debt-to-Asset Ratio Interpretation Action
Under 20% Excellent — minimal leverage, most assets truly yours Maintain current approach, focus on growth
20-30% Healthy — common for homeowners with small bonds Continue paying down debt while investing
30-50% Moderate — typical for homeowners with active bonds Monitor, consider accelerating bond payments
50-70% High leverage — large portion of assets financed Prioritise debt reduction, avoid new debt
Over 70% Concerning — highly leveraged, vulnerable to shocks Aggressive debt payoff plan essential
Over 100% Negative net worth territory Emergency debt reduction, possibly debt review

Good debt vs bad debt

Not all debt is equal. A home loan at prime (currently ~10.5%) financing an appreciating asset is very different from credit card debt at 22% financing depreciating purchases:

  • Acceptable debt: Home loans, student loans (if degree increases earning potential), business loans for profitable businesses
  • Tolerable debt: Car finance (necessary for most South Africans), but pay off quickly
  • Toxic debt: Credit cards, store accounts, personal loans for consumption, payday loans — eliminate these urgently

Why net worth can (and should) be negative early on

Many people, particularly those who have recently bought a home, taken on a student loan, or financed a car, have negative net worth for a period — this is normal and not itself a crisis. A 28-year-old with a R1.5 million bond on a R1.8 million property, a R200,000 car loan, and R100,000 in student debt has negative net worth despite being on a sound financial path.

What matters far more than the number at any single point is the trend: is your net worth growing over each measurement period, even from a negative starting point?

The negative net worth recovery pattern

Most South Africans who build substantial wealth pass through a negative net worth phase. The typical recovery pattern:

  1. Student debt years (22-28): Negative net worth from student loans
  2. First property (28-35): Still often negative due to bond exceeding early equity
  3. Equity building (35-45): Property appreciation + bond reduction moves to positive
  4. Wealth acceleration (45+): Compound growth on investments accelerates wealth
The key insight: Don't judge your financial health by a single net worth number in your 20s or early 30s. Judge it by whether the number is improving each year. A 30-year-old moving from -R300,000 to -R200,000 is making excellent progress.

The three levers of net worth growth

There are fundamentally only three ways to grow your net worth — every financial decision fits into one of these categories:

1. Increase income (earn more)

Higher income provides more fuel for saving and investing. This happens through promotions, job changes, side hustles, business growth, or upskilling. Income growth alone doesn't build wealth — only the portion saved contributes — but it provides more raw material to work with.

2. Decrease spending (save more of what you earn)

The gap between income and spending is what becomes wealth. Reducing spending without reducing income directly increases savings available for investing. This is often the fastest lever — cutting R5,000/month in expenses and investing it at 10% creates R1 million in 12 years.

3. Increase investment returns (grow what you've saved)

Better investment returns compound your savings faster. This happens through asset allocation, minimising fees, tax efficiency, and time in market. However, chasing returns often increases risk — consistent moderate returns usually beat volatile high returns.

The South African wealth-building priority stack

  1. Emergency fund: 3-6 months expenses in accessible account
  2. High-interest debt: Pay off credit cards, store accounts, personal loans over 18%
  3. Employer retirement match: Always take free money
  4. Maximise TFSA: R40,000/year, tax-free forever
  5. Maximise RA: 27.5% of income tax deduction (max R350k)
  6. Additional investments: Direct ETFs, shares, property
  7. Accelerate bond payments: Once other priorities are met

The tax-advantaged vehicles every South African should use

Retirement Annuities (RA)

Contributions are tax-deductible up to 27.5% of taxable income (maximum R350,000/year). For someone in the 36% marginal tax bracket, a R100,000 RA contribution saves R36,000 in tax — effectively a 36% instant return before any market growth. The trade-off is inaccessibility until age 55.

Tax-Free Savings Accounts (TFSA)

You can contribute R40,000/year (R500,000 lifetime) with all growth, dividends, and withdrawals completely tax-free. Over 20 years at 10% growth, R40,000/year becomes R2.4 million — entirely tax-free. This is one of the most powerful wealth-building tools available to South Africans and should be maximised every year.

Pension and Provident Funds

Employer retirement funds offer tax-deductible contributions (combined with RA up to 27.5% limit) plus often include employer matching contributions. Always contribute at least enough to get the full employer match — it's free money.

Making net worth tracking a habit

Net worth is most valuable tracked consistently — quarterly is a common, manageable rhythm. A single measurement is a snapshot; a series of measurements over time is a trend line that shows whether your financial decisions are compounding in the right direction.

The quarterly tracking ritual

  1. Choose your date: Same date each quarter (e.g., 1 Jan, 1 Apr, 1 Jul, 1 Oct)
  2. Gather statements: Bank, investment, retirement, loan statements
  3. Get property value: Check recent sales on Property24 or get bank valuation
  4. Get vehicle value: TransUnion Auto Value or WeBuyCars estimate
  5. Run the calculation: Use this calculator or your spreadsheet
  6. Compare to last quarter: Is the number growing? By how much?
  7. Review allocation: Is your asset mix still appropriate?
  8. Set next quarter's focus: What one action will most improve your position?

What to look for in trends

  • Consistent growth: Net worth should generally increase each quarter, though setbacks happen
  • Rate of growth: Are you building wealth faster or slower than before?
  • Composition shifts: Is your asset allocation moving toward more productive assets?
  • Debt reduction: Is your debt-to-asset ratio improving?
  • Seasonal patterns: December bonuses, annual increases — factor these in

Tools for tracking over time

  • 22seven: South African app that aggregates accounts and tracks net worth automatically
  • Spreadsheet: Simple Google Sheet or Excel with quarterly entries
  • Banking apps: FNB, Standard Bank, Discovery offer net worth tracking
  • Personal Capital-type tools: Some local alternatives emerging
  • Financial adviser: Many provide quarterly net worth reports as part of service

Common net worth calculation mistakes

  • Using purchase price for property: Always use current market value — property may be worth more or less than you paid
  • Using purchase price for vehicles: Cars depreciate 15-20% annually, use current market value
  • Forgetting retirement savings: Pension/RA balances are often your largest asset
  • Excluding "small" debts: Store accounts and credit cards add up quickly
  • Counting household goods: Furniture and appliances aren't real assets — they depreciate rapidly and aren't liquid
  • Inflating business value: Unless you have a recent offer, be conservative
  • Ignoring crypto volatility: Include at current value but understand it can swing 50%+ in months
  • Not tracking consistently: One calculation is a snapshot — you need a series to see trends
  • Comparing to wrong benchmarks: Compare to similar age/income, not social media highlight reels
  • Judging by single number: The trend matters more than any snapshot

The psychology of net worth

Tracking net worth isn't just mathematical — it's psychological. Understanding the emotional aspects helps you stay the course:

The motivation effect

Seeing your net worth grow is genuinely motivating. Many people who start tracking become more intentional about financial decisions because they can see the cumulative impact. A R10,000 unnecessary purchase isn't just R10,000 — it's R10,000 less net worth that could have been compounding.

The comparison trap

Social media makes comparison inevitable but usually unhealthy. People post their wins, not their debt. A 30-year-old "millionaire" might have R5 million in assets and R4.5 million in debt — net worth R500,000. Compare to your past self, not others' curated presentations.

The market volatility challenge

Investment values fluctuate daily. A 10% market drop can temporarily reduce your net worth by hundreds of thousands — without you doing anything wrong. This is why quarterly tracking beats daily obsessing. Stay focused on long-term trends, not daily noise.

The lifestyle inflation danger

As income grows, spending often grows proportionally — a phenomenon called lifestyle inflation. This keeps net worth flat despite higher earnings. The antidote: commit to saving at least half of every raise or bonus, letting lifestyle improvements lag income growth.

Advanced net worth concepts

Liquid vs illiquid net worth

Your total net worth includes both liquid assets (cash, investments you can access quickly) and illiquid assets (property, business value, locked retirement funds). For financial resilience, focus on building liquid net worth — money you can access within days without penalty. A common guideline is to have 6-12 months of expenses in liquid form.

Tangible vs intangible assets

Traditional net worth focuses on tangible assets — things with market value. But intangible assets matter too: your skills, education, professional network, reputation, and health all contribute to your earning power and financial resilience. While harder to quantify, investing in these often provides excellent returns.

Household vs individual net worth

For couples, tracking household net worth (combined assets and debts) gives the full picture. However, understanding individual net worth can also be valuable, especially in community of property vs out of community marriages. Discuss with your partner which approach makes sense for your situation.

South African wealth statistics

Understanding where you fit in the broader South African context provides perspective:

Metric South Africa 2026 Notes
Median household income R10,000-R15,000/month Half earn less, half earn more
Average household income R25,000-R35,000/month Skewed high by top earners
Home ownership rate ~65% Varies dramatically by income group
Adults with retirement savings ~45% Most have inadequate retirement savings
Adults with emergency savings (3+ months) ~25% Most vulnerable to financial shocks
Households with any investments ~30% Excludes retirement funds

The inequality reality: South Africa has extreme wealth inequality. The top 10% own approximately 80% of the country's wealth. This means "average" figures are heavily skewed — median (the middle point) is often more representative of typical experience.

When to seek professional financial advice

Certain situations warrant professional guidance from a registered financial adviser (FSCA-registered):

  • Net worth over R5 million: Tax planning and estate planning become important
  • Approaching retirement (10 years out): Professional retirement planning essential
  • Complex tax situation: Multiple income sources, investments, business ownership
  • Inheritance or windfall: Large sums require careful planning
  • Business ownership: Business value and succession planning
  • International assets: Exchange control and cross-border tax implications
  • Debt over R500,000 (excluding bond): May benefit from structured debt plan

Look for a fee-based adviser rather than commission-based — their advice is more likely to be objective and aligned with your interests.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is net worth?

Net worth is the total value of everything you own (assets) minus everything you owe (liabilities/debts). It is the single best measure of your overall financial health — more informative than income because it reflects what you have actually built, not just what you earn.

What is a good net worth in South Africa?

A useful benchmark is your age times your annual income divided by ten. So at age 40 earning R500,000/year, a good net worth would be around R2 million. However, building positive, growing net worth over time matters more than any single number.

What is the average net worth in South Africa by age?

Approximate South African averages for middle-income households: Age 25-34: R50,000-R200,000; Age 35-44: R300,000-R800,000; Age 45-54: R1 million-R3 million; Age 55-64: R2 million-R5 million. These vary significantly by income level and home ownership.

Should I include my pension or RA in net worth?

Yes, absolutely. Your pension, provident fund, and retirement annuity balances are real assets — often the largest asset for middle-income South Africans. Include the current fund value shown on your latest benefit statement.

Should I include my car in net worth?

Yes, but at current market value, not purchase price. Cars depreciate 15-20% annually in SA. Use trade-in or private sale value from TransUnion Auto Value or WeBuyCars for realistic estimates.

Is negative net worth normal?

Yes, particularly for younger South Africans with student loans, new home bonds, or car finance. What matters is the trend — is your net worth growing each year? Many successful professionals had negative net worth until their mid-30s.

What is a healthy debt-to-asset ratio?

Under 30% is considered healthy — most of what you own is genuinely yours. 30-60% is moderate and common for homeowners with bonds. Above 60% suggests high leverage. Above 80% is concerning and warrants prioritising debt reduction.

Should I include stokvel savings?

Yes. Stokvels are legitimate savings vehicles. Include your current stokvel balance as a cash/savings asset. They often provide discipline that individual savings lack.

How often should I calculate net worth?

Quarterly is ideal — frequent enough to spot trends, infrequent enough to avoid obsessing over daily market fluctuations. Track on the same date each quarter.

How should I value my home?

Use realistic current market value, not purchase price or municipal valuation. Check recent sales of similar properties on Property24 or get a free bank valuation. Be conservative.

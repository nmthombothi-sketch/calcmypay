Debt Snowball vs Avalanche Method

Jun 20265 min readMoney Tools

Two popular strategies dominate debt payoff: the snowball and the avalanche. Both work — the best one is the one you will actually stick to. Here is the difference.

The avalanche method

Pay minimums on everything, then throw all extra money at your highest interest rate debt first. Mathematically optimal — it minimises total interest paid. Best if you are motivated by saving money.

The snowball method

Pay minimums on everything, then attack your smallest balance first. You clear debts quickly, which builds momentum and motivation. Best if you need psychological wins to stay on track.

Which saves more?

The avalanche always saves at least as much interest as the snowball. But studies show many people stick with the snowball better because of the early wins — and a method you follow beats an optimal one you abandon.

The honest recommendation

If the interest-rate gaps between your debts are large, use the avalanche — the savings are worth it. If your debts are similar in rate, or you have struggled to stay motivated before, use the snowball. Run both through the debt repayment calculator to see the difference for your situation.

A hybrid approach that some find works best

Some people find success combining elements of both methods — using avalanche logic for genuinely high-interest debt like credit cards, while allowing a small, quick snowball win on a minor account early on to build momentum and confidence before settling into the more mathematically efficient approach for the remainder.

Tracking progress to sustain motivation either way

Regardless of method chosen, visually tracking your remaining balances — a simple spreadsheet or even a printed chart — provides tangible evidence of progress that pure numbers in a banking app often fail to convey, which matters more for sustained motivation than people initially expect.

When professional debt counselling becomes the better path

If total debt repayments consistently exceed what your income can sustainably manage even with an optimised method, self-directed snowball or avalanche approaches may not be sufficient. At that point, formal debt counselling through South Africa's National Credit Regulator can restructure your obligations into something genuinely manageable.

The mathematics behind why avalanche saves more

The avalanche method targets your highest-interest debt first with any extra payment, which minimises the total interest accumulated across your entire debt load over time — a debt at 24% interest costs meaningfully more per rand of balance than one at 12%, so eliminating the expensive debt first reduces the total cost fastest, regardless of the size of either balance.

Why snowball still works for many people

Behavioural research on debt repayment consistently shows that visible, frequent progress — completely eliminating an account, not just reducing a large balance — meaningfully improves the odds that people stick with a repayment plan to completion. The snowball method sacrifices some mathematical optimality for psychological momentum, which for many people translates into actually finishing the plan rather than abandoning it partway through.

A hybrid approach worth considering

Some people combine both methods: using avalanche logic for debts with a significant interest rate difference (where the savings are substantial), but switching to snowball logic when two debts have similar rates, since the interest-cost difference becomes negligible and the motivational benefit of a quick win takes priority.

A hybrid approach some people find effective

Rather than choosing purely one method, some successfully combine them — using the avalanche method's logic for their largest, highest-interest debts while allowing themselves one small "quick win" debt cleared first using snowball logic, capturing some early motivation without abandoning the more mathematically efficient overall strategy.

A hybrid approach some people find works best

Rather than choosing purely one method, some people apply the avalanche method to debts with meaningfully higher interest rates (where the maths clearly favours prioritisation) while using snowball logic to decide the order among debts with similar rates, capturing both the mathematical efficiency and the psychological benefit of the two approaches.

Why the interest rate matters more than the balance

A common instinct is to attack the largest debt balance first, but this is usually the wrong strategy financially. A R50,000 balance at 8% interest costs you far less over time than a R15,000 balance at 24% — and every month you delay paying down the high-interest debt, it compounds against you. Prioritising by interest rate, not balance, is what actually minimises what you pay in total.

The math is clear: On a R50,000 credit card balance at 22% paying only R1,500/month, you'll pay R67,000 in interest over 7+ years. Put the same payment against a R50,000 car loan at 13% and you pay only R15,000 in interest over 3 years. Interest rate is the multiplier that determines how expensive a debt truly is.

The debt avalanche versus debt snowball trade-off

Method How it works Best for
Avalanche Pay minimums on everything, extra to highest interest rate first Minimising total interest paid — mathematically optimal
Snowball Pay minimums on everything, extra to smallest balance first Building momentum through quick wins — better for motivation

Both methods reach debt freedom, but the avalanche method genuinely saves more money in total interest. The snowball method sacrifices some of that saving in exchange for psychological wins that keep people consistent — and consistency is what ultimately determines success, so neither approach is objectively wrong. Pick the one you will actually stick with.

The real cost of only paying minimums

Credit card minimum payments are typically structured so that a large portion goes to interest rather than principal in the early years. Paying only the minimum on a R30,000 balance at 22% interest can take over 15 years to clear and cost more than double the original balance in interest alone — a stark illustration of why extra payments matter so much.

Debt consolidation: when it helps and when it hurts

Debt consolidation — combining multiple debts into a single loan, often at a lower rate — can genuinely help if the new rate is meaningfully lower and you do not use the freed-up credit to accumulate new debt. It becomes harmful when it simply extends your repayment timeline without reducing your total interest, or when old credit facilities are left open and get used again. Consolidation without a change in spending habits often leaves people worse off within a year or two.

The consolidation trap: Research consistently shows that 70%+ of people who consolidate credit card debt run those cards back up within 2 years — ending up with both the consolidation loan and fresh card balances. Close old facilities when you consolidate, or the lower rate becomes a trap rather than a tool.

Debt review under the National Credit Act

If you are genuinely over-indebted — meaning you cannot meet your monthly obligations across all your debts — South Africa's National Credit Act provides a formal debt review process (often called debt counselling). A registered debt counsellor negotiates with your creditors to reduce your monthly payments and often your interest rates, and you receive legal protection from repossession and legal action while under review.

Debt review is worth considering when:

  • You are consistently missing or late on payments
  • You are receiving letters of demand or section 129 notices
  • You are using one credit facility to pay another
  • You have no realistic path to clearing debts within 5 years on current terms

The real costs of debt review:

  • You cannot take on new credit while under review
  • Fees apply: Application fee (~R50), counselling fee, restructuring fee, and ongoing after-care fee (typically 2.5-5% of each payment)
  • Credit record impact: Your record is flagged for the duration of review (typically 3-5 years)
  • Duration: Usually 3-5 years of reduced payments

Debt review is a legitimate, legislated safety net — not a scam. But it is a serious step that should be taken only when genuine over-indebtedness exists, not as a shortcut to lower payments for people who could otherwise manage their debts with discipline.

Prescribed debt — a protection many South Africans don't know about

Under the Prescription Act (and reinforced by the National Credit Act for credit agreements), most consumer debts prescribe after 3 years if no payment has been made and no legal action has been taken. Once prescribed, a debt is legally unenforceable — creditors cannot collect it through the courts or list it negatively with credit bureaus.

What prescribes after 3 years:

  • Credit card debt
  • Personal loans
  • Retail/store accounts
  • Cellphone contracts
  • Gym memberships
  • Medical accounts (private)

What takes 30 years to prescribe:

  • Home loans (mortgage bonds)
  • Judgment debts (court-ordered)
  • Tax debts owed to SARS
Critical warning: Making even a small payment, or acknowledging the debt in writing, resets the prescription clock. Debt collectors often contact consumers about prescribed debts hoping for exactly this response. If contacted about an old debt, ask for written proof it has not prescribed before acknowledging it or paying anything.

What to do once debt is cleared

The moment a debt is paid off, the temptation is to absorb that freed-up monthly amount into general spending. Instead, redirect it immediately — either to the next debt on your list, or once debt-free, into your emergency fund and then long-term investing. That redirected payment is often the single biggest lever available for accelerating your financial progress.

This is the secret behind people who go from heavily indebted to financially secure within a decade: they don't stop paying when the debt is gone. They keep paying — just into different accounts that build wealth instead of servicing interest.

The psychology of debt payoff

Paying off debt is as much a psychological challenge as a mathematical one. Months of discipline with seemingly slow progress can erode motivation. Three tactics that consistently help:

  • Visual tracking: A thermometer chart on your fridge, a spreadsheet with a progress bar — seeing the balance drop physically sustains motivation.
  • Celebrate milestones: Every R10,000 paid off, every debt fully cleared — mark the moment. Small celebrations prevent burnout.
  • Automate the extra: Set up a recurring additional payment that goes out the day after your salary arrives, before lifestyle spending can absorb it.

Common South African debt mistakes

  • Using credit for lifestyle: Credit cards for holidays, clothes, and entertainment create debts that fund depreciating experiences at 22% interest.
  • Payment holidays: Skipping a payment "just this once" costs thousands in additional interest and breaks momentum.
  • Balance transfers without a plan: Moving debt between cards buys time but doesn't reduce the total. Use the breathing room to pay aggressively.
  • Ignoring letters of demand: Under the NCA, a section 129 notice is a formal step before legal action. Responding within the required timeframe preserves your rights.
  • Paying prescribed debts: Collection agents regularly try to collect prescribed debts. Verify before paying anything.

Where to get legitimate help in South Africa

  • National Credit Regulator: www.ncr.org.za — oversees the credit industry and handles complaints
  • National Debt Mediation Association: Free counselling on debt review options
  • Credit bureaus: Experian, TransUnion, XDS, Compuscan — request your free annual credit report
  • Legal Aid South Africa: 0800 110 110 — free legal help for those who qualify
  • Registered debt counsellors: Verify registration on the NCR website before engaging

Why the dealer's number is too high

Dealers calculate the maximum the bank will approve — not what is healthy for your budget. Approval often allows 20–25% of income on a car. Living at that level for 6 years leaves little for saving, and any income shock becomes a crisis.

Match the term to the car

A 72-month loan lowers the monthly but means you pay far more interest and often owe more than the car is worth for years. Where possible, choose 60 months or less and a car you can comfortably afford on that term. See your take-home first with the salary calculator.

Matching a car repayment to your income realistically

Car dealers and finance companies will typically approve a loan based on what the bank believes you can technically afford, which is often a higher figure than what is genuinely sensible for your overall financial health. The 15% guideline used throughout this platform is more conservative than typical bank approval criteria specifically because it leaves room for the other costs of car ownership and life beyond the vehicle.

Why the repayment is not the full affordability picture

A repayment that fits comfortably within 15% of income still needs to sit alongside insurance, fuel and maintenance without pushing your total transport cost above roughly 20-25% of take-home pay. Two people with identical repayments can have very different overall affordability depending on their commute distance and insurance costs.

The deposit effect on affordability

A meaningful deposit — even 10-15% of the purchase price — proportionally increases what total vehicle price becomes affordable within the same monthly repayment budget, since less needs to be financed. It also typically reduces the interest rate offered, compounding the benefit further.

The 15% guideline as your starting anchor

Most South African financial advisers recommend keeping your total car repayment under 15% of your gross monthly income — a threshold designed to leave adequate room for the genuinely substantial additional costs of vehicle ownership: insurance, fuel, maintenance, and licensing, which together often equal or exceed the repayment itself.

Why the loan term dramatically changes the outcome

The same monthly repayment can finance a meaningfully more expensive car over 72 months than over 48 months — but at a real cost. A 72-month term typically means paying 40-60% more in total interest than a 48-month term on an equivalent loan amount, and leaves you owing more than the car is worth for a much longer period.

Salary increases and car upgrades: a common trap

It is tempting to upgrade your vehicle every time your income rises, treating the 15% ceiling as a target to fill rather than a maximum to stay under. Sustained wealth building often depends on resisting this specific form of lifestyle inflation — keeping a car payment well below 15%, or eliminating it entirely once a vehicle is paid off, frees substantial monthly cash flow for saving and investing instead.

The case for buying below your ceiling

Financing a car meaningfully below your 15% ceiling — say, at 8-10% of income — creates a genuine buffer against income disruption and frees immediate cash flow for other financial goals. The car that "just barely fits" your budget at 15% leaves no room for a bad month, an income gap, or an unexpected expense arriving in the same period as a large repayment.

The 15% guideline, and why it protects more than just your monthly budget

Keeping your car repayment under 15% of gross income is not simply about affordability in isolation — it protects your capacity to save, handle emergencies, and absorb an interest rate increase without the vehicle becoming a genuine financial strain. Vehicles financed at the very edge of what a bank will approve frequently become the first thing that breaks a household budget when circumstances change.

Why dealership pre-approval figures are not a safe target

Dealership finance pre-approvals typically reflect what a bank will lend based on your income and existing debt, often allowing 20-25% of income toward a vehicle — a figure calculated for the bank's risk tolerance, not your personal financial comfort. Treating a pre-approval amount as your target, rather than as an upper ceiling to stay well below, is one of the most common ways people end up with an uncomfortably tight budget.

The interest rate sensitivity worth understanding

A vehicle financed near the edge of affordability at today's interest rate can become genuinely unaffordable if rates rise during the loan term, particularly on variable-rate finance agreements. Building in a buffer below the maximum you technically qualify for provides real protection against this scenario, which has affected many South African vehicle owners during past rate-hiking cycles.

Matching the loan term to the vehicle, not just the payment

A longer term reduces the monthly figure but increases total interest paid and extends the period during which you owe more than the vehicle is worth. Where your budget allows, a shorter term — even if it means financing a slightly less expensive vehicle — is usually the financially sounder choice.

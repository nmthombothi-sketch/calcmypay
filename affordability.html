How to know what you can really afford

The problem with "how much can I get approved for?"

Banks and dealers will often approve you for more than you should comfortably spend. Just because you qualify for a bigger bond or car loan doesn't mean it fits your life. Our affordability tools use the same guidelines banks use — the 30% rule for housing, the 15% rule for vehicles — but applied honestly, with your full financial picture in mind, not just what maximises what you can borrow.

The four numbers that matter most

  • Home affordability — the price range your salary genuinely supports on a 20-year bond, before you even start house-hunting.
  • Car affordability — what repayment keeps your vehicle costs sane, including the running costs most people forget to budget for.
  • Rent affordability — a comfortable monthly rent using the widely accepted 30% guideline, so you are not house-poor from day one.
  • Lifestyle affordability — whether your total spending, across everything, actually fits your income with room to save.

Why this matters more than the loan approval

An approval is the bank's risk assessment, not yours. It tells you what they are willing to lend, not what leaves you able to save, handle an emergency, or sleep at night. These four tools exist to answer the question that actually matters: not "can I get this," but "should I."

Common affordability mistakes to avoid

Using pre-approval as a target, not a ceiling. Pre-approval reflects the maximum a bank will risk, calculated on their terms — not the amount that keeps your life comfortable. Treat it as an upper bound you should rarely approach, not a goal.

Forgetting the costs beyond the repayment. A bond needs rates, levies, insurance and maintenance on top. A car needs insurance, fuel and services on top. Our tools flag these, but it is worth budgeting for them explicitly before you commit.

Ignoring how affordability changes over time. An affordable repayment today can become tight after a rate hike, a job change, or a new dependant. Build in a margin, not just a bare minimum.

Why "can I afford it" needs more than a gut feeling

Affordability questions — for a home, a car, or your everyday lifestyle — are some of the highest-stakes financial decisions most people make, yet they are frequently answered by instinct or by whatever a bank pre-approves, rather than a clear framework. The tools on this page apply consistent, transparent rules to give you an honest answer before you commit.

The common thread across every affordability calculation

Whether you are evaluating a home, a car, or rent, the underlying logic is the same: take your income, apply a sensible percentage ceiling for that category of spending, and work out what that translates to in real terms. The percentages differ by category because different types of spending carry different risk and flexibility, but the discipline of thinking in percentages rather than absolute rand amounts is what keeps every affordability decision grounded.

  • Housing (rent or bond): generally capped around 30% of gross income
  • Vehicle: generally capped around 15% of gross income for the repayment alone
  • Overall lifestyle: the sum of all your commitments should still leave room for saving

Why banks approving you does not mean you should spend the maximum

A bank's approval reflects their risk tolerance and lending criteria, not your personal financial goals. Banks can often approve larger bonds or car finance than is genuinely healthy for your broader financial picture — approval is a ceiling to stay under, not a target to reach.

How these four tools work together

Home, car, rent and lifestyle affordability are not independent questions — committing to a large bond affects how much car you can sensibly afford, and vice versa. Running your numbers through more than one of these tools, ideally together, gives a far more realistic picture than checking any single one in isolation.

Making an affordability decision you will not regret

The healthiest affordability decisions leave a buffer — for job changes, interest rate increases, or unexpected expenses — rather than stretching to the exact maximum a calculator suggests. Use these tools to establish your ceiling, then aim comfortably below it, particularly for large, long-term commitments like a home loan.
